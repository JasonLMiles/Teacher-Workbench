# Teacher Workbench Literacy Instruction

## Start
Read [grounding and citations](references/grounding-and-citations.md) for every invocation. Read [coordination and capabilities](references/coordination.md) for the handoff and host-specific fallback rules. Use the [source directory](references/source-directory.md) to locate relevant authoritative sources; retrieve the actual source before citing its claims. Apply only the foundations relevant to the teacher's decision.

## Inputs
Required: grade/course, literacy goal and available text or task. Optional: actual reading/writing evidence, language strengths, decoding/fluency observations, curriculum, time and documented supports.

## Workflow
1. Identify the component and evidence: print/phonological/decoding skills, fluency, language, background knowledge, comprehension, composition or transcription. Do not treat low comprehension scores as proof of a decoding problem or an English-proficiency label as a complete reading profile.
2. Verify relevant California ELA/ELD standards and framework chapter. Match WWC evidence to scope: foundational reading guide K–3, reading intervention guide grades 4–9, or the English learner guide where appropriate. Read the recommendation itself and retain its rating.
3. Design an explicit short routine: model/think aloud, guided practice with response opportunities, supported application, independent check and transfer into meaningful reading or writing. Select content-rich, accessible text and explain why its demands fit.
4. Make a model answer or annotated writing example and explain the visible moves. For decoding work, attend to the actual sound–spelling pattern rather than teaching guessing from pictures as word identification. For comprehension, use evidence from text and knowledge without reducing reading to isolated strategy labels.
5. Adapt language, access and challenge without removing the intended literacy construct. A read-aloud may help content access but changes evidence when independent decoding is the target. Coordinate with multilingual and differentiation skills where needed.
6. Use a brief transfer task and actual errors to choose the next step. Keep diagnostic observations tentative; offer a targeted follow-up rather than labeling a disability.

## Deliver
Return the routine, text/material requirements, teacher model, student practice, key or criteria, access/extension pathways and a short evidence check with cited rationale.

## Limits
Do not diagnose dyslexia or prescribe services. Verify permissions for reproduced text. Label original practice passages as authored examples and do not present them as published literature.

## Routing examples
Direct: “Build a grade 4 main-idea lesson from this text.” Overlap: newcomer literacy → multilingual skill checks language demands and home-language literacy. Boundary: clinical diagnosis → observations and appropriate assessment referral.
