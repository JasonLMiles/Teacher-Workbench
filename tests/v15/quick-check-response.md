# Three-minute area check and one teaching rehearsal

**Grade 3 · Proposed original materials based on the supplied area lesson**

The check asks whether students can connect a rectangle's square units to equal rows or columns and multiplication. Use it just after the 3-by-5 model to decide whether to move into the supplied rectangle comparison or briefly return to counting and grouping squares. No actual student responses were supplied; the patterns below are hypotheses to investigate.

**Lesson source:** [Area lesson — supplied sample for resource development](cases/lesson.md), unpaginated; model, practice, exit and required-support lines. The lesson provides 18 minutes, paper, grid paper and pencils, oral directions and pointing responses. Students do not use AI.

## Student check — copy this section only

**Directions:** You have about three minutes. You may write, draw, say your answer or point to show your thinking. Include what the numbers count.

**Rectangle A:** A rectangle is covered completely by **2 rows of unit squares, with 5 unit squares in each row**. There are no gaps or overlaps. Each small square has an area of 1 square unit.

**1. Find the area.**

What is the area of Rectangle A? Include the unit. Write or say a multiplication equation that matches the rectangle. You may point to the numbers as someone records your equation.

Area: ____________________

Equation: ____________________

**2. Explain the counting.**

Mina says, “The area is 7 square units because 2 + 5 = 7.”

Do you agree? Use the rows, columns or individual squares to show why. Explain or point to what each number in your multiplication equation counts.

My thinking: ____________________________________________________

## Teacher setup and key — keep separate

Before the check, outline a rectangle **2 grid squares tall and 5 grid squares wide**, retaining every internal grid line. Provide it on each student's paper or large enough to point to. This is the exact diagram specification for Rectangle A; the student text also supplies its full structure. Do not fill in the area or equation.

Read the directions and both questions aloud. Suggested allocation: 20 seconds for directions, 60 seconds for item 1, 80 seconds for item 2, and 20 seconds to collect or note responses. This is a planning estimate, not a speed criterion. Preserve any required response time. A single teacher may need to finish recording individual pointing responses during the next practice period; do not mark an unobserved response incorrect.

| Item | Checked answer and acceptable reasoning | Evidence to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **10 square units.** Accept **2 × 5 = 10** or **5 × 2 = 10** with a matching explanation. There are two rows of five squares or five columns of two squares. | Total; square-unit label; multiplication equation. A correct equation alone does not show what the factors mean. |
| 2 | **Disagree.** Adding the two side counts gives 7, but does not count all the squares covering the rectangle. Two groups of five give **5 + 5 = 10**, equivalent to **2 × 5 = 10**. Five groups of two are equally valid. | Student identifies both equal-group quantities and connects them to the full covered region. Accept pointing across both rows or down all five columns with an unambiguous mapping to the equation. |

Count-all reasoning correctly establishes the area. If the student counts ten squares but cannot yet connect that count to multiplication, record the area understanding and the multiplication connection separately. Do not reject a correct column method because the model used rows.

## Responses to instruction

The prompts and materials below are proposed teaching moves. Use a brief probe before deciding what an error means. The square-unit and row connection follows the [California Mathematics Framework, chapter 6, pp. 83–84](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ci/ma/cf/documents/mathframeworkch6.pdf); the precise items and timing are locally designed.

| Observed response or reasoning | Tentative interpretation and distinguishing probe | Exact next teaching move or practice | Independent check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 square units, valid equation, and clear row/column mapping | Evidence of the target on this example. Ask, “How does your equation count every square?” to distinguish a remembered fact from a represented product. | Move into the supplied practice: “Draw a rectangle 2 units tall and 6 units wide, and another 3 units tall and 4 units wide. Compare their areas. Show why.” Both cover 12 square units. | Use F1 below, then retain the supplied 4-by-5 exit. |
| 7, with 2 + 5 as the reason | Consistent with adding side counts; not proof of a stable misconception. Ask, “Point to everything you counted in your 2 and your 5.” | On a fresh **2-by-3** grid, say, “Touch every square inside exactly once. Now group the squares by rows. How many groups? How many in each?” Connect **3 + 3 = 6** to **2 × 3 = 6** after the student counts. | F1: look for 18 square units and a factor-to-group explanation. |
| 10 from counting individual squares, but no equation or “I just counted” | Area counting is present; the grouping connection is not yet demonstrated. Ask, “Show one group that repeats in this rectangle.” | On the **2-by-3** grid, have the student mark the two rows, record **3 + 3**, and make a multiplication equation that counts the same squares. | F1: accept rows or columns, but require the student to map both factors. |
| A correct product with an unclear explanation, or a wrong product despite a correctly described arrangement | Could reflect a remembered rule, an arithmetic slip or difficulty communicating. Ask, “Show each factor on the grid, then count the squares to check your total.” | If grouping is correct, have the student record a running row total and check the product. If grouping is unclear, use the 2-by-3 grouping move above. Record which evidence resolved the uncertainty. | F1: compare the equation, represented groups and counted total. |
| Correct count/equation, but “10 units” or no unit | The label may be omitted even when area is understood. Ask, “Point to what one of your ten measures.” | If the student points to a whole square, name **one square unit**, have the student label the total, and distinguish it from one edge of that square. If the student traces boundary edges, return to covering the inside of the 2-by-3 grid. | F1: student identifies a whole unit square and labels the total. |
| Blank, unfinished, or an ambiguous pointing response | No interpretable evidence yet; possible time, access or task-language issue. | Read the task again without explaining the operation. Offer the prepared grid and ask the student to show their answer in their preferred permitted mode. Record the words and gestures actually observed. | Administer F1 when the student can access it; leave the initial result as missing evidence. |

**Decision:** Record separate evidence for area, multiplication meaning and units. Continue to comparison for students who demonstrate all three; use the relevant short move for those whose evidence is incomplete. If the same unresolved issue appears across the room, make that move a brief shared revisit; otherwise use it during practice. This is a proposed instructional rule, with no class percentage or mastery cut score. If reporting a class count, give the number with interpretable responses as the denominator and list missing responses separately. Do not form permanent groups from this check.

## F1 — parallel follow-up after the teaching move

Provide a fresh grid rectangle **3 squares tall and 6 squares wide**, with all internal lines visible. Do not display the worked 2-by-3 example beside it.

**Read to the student:** “This rectangle has 3 rows with 6 unit squares in each row. What is its area? Include the unit. Write or say a multiplication equation. Use the grid to explain what each factor counts.”

**Key:** **18 square units; 3 × 6 = 18** with three rows of six, or **6 × 3 = 18** with six columns of three. Accept matching pointing, drawing or oral explanations. Record the same three evidence dimensions. Oral directions and pointing remain available; remove only the optional worked example and corrective hints. If the explanation still needs prompts, record that support and revisit the target in the next lesson rather than declaring mastery or failure.

Retain the supplied exit: a **4-by-5** rectangle has area **20 square units**, shown as four rows of five or five columns of four. F1 and the exit give another sample; a change in these brief responses does not establish that a particular teaching move caused learning.

## Access, interpretation and teacher sources

Oral directions and pointing are required in the supplied lesson and stay in the check, teaching moves and follow-up. Read the words without emphasizing a correct operation. For pointing, record what the student indicates; confirm an ambiguous gesture neutrally. A scribed response must reflect the student's own selection or reasoning. These conditions elicit mathematical understanding without requiring handwriting or independent reading; they do not establish independent written explanation. Do not subtract credit for required supports.

These are teacher-authored-style draft materials, not standardized or psychometrically calibrated items. A brief sample cannot establish a disability, intervention tier, placement, durable mastery or the cause of an error. No real learner evidence has been analyzed.

## Interactive rehearsal — your turn

**Focus:** Elicit the meaning behind a correct multiplication answer during the supplied model.

**Preparation card:** The model is 3 unit squares tall and 5 unit squares wide. Its checked area is **15 square units**: three rows of five or five columns of three. You have the grid and a pencil. Listen for the student connecting both factors to groups of square units; students retain the counting and explaining. Allow oral or pointing responses. ### Rehearsal continuation

**Teacher's actual attempt:**

> “The 3 means three rows, the 5 means five in each row, and three times five is fifteen. Repeat that back to me.”

**Focused feedback:** Your row model and calculation are accurate, and the wording is concise. When naming the area, include **15 square units**. You supplied both factor meanings before the student located them; “Repeat that back” could show recall of your words without showing the picture-to-equation connection. The next move can invite the student's own pointing and reasoning.

**One revision to try:** “Use your finger to show what each number in 3 × 5 counts in this picture.”

**Retry:** Return to the same simulated student, who knows the product but cannot yet explain the factors. What would you say next in one or two sentences? Include any gesture you would use, while leaving the factor meanings for the student to show.
